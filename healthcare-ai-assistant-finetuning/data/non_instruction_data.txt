A fever is a temporary rise in body temperature, usually a sign that the body is fighting an infection. For most adults, a temperature at or above 38 degrees Celsius (100.4 degrees Fahrenheit) is considered a fever. Mild fevers are often part of a normal immune response and do not always require medication. Rest, fluids, and monitoring are usually enough, but a fever above 39.4 degrees Celsius, or one that lasts more than three days, should be evaluated by a healthcare professional.

The common cold is a viral infection of the nose and throat. Typical symptoms include a runny or stuffy nose, sneezing, sore throat, mild cough, and low-grade fever. Most colds resolve on their own within seven to ten days. Because colds are caused by viruses, antibiotics do not help and should not be used. Supportive care such as hydration, rest, and over-the-counter symptom relief is the usual approach.

Influenza, commonly called the flu, is a respiratory illness caused by influenza viruses. Unlike a cold, the flu often comes on suddenly with high fever, body aches, fatigue, and a dry cough. The flu can lead to serious complications in older adults, young children, pregnant people, and those with chronic conditions. An annual flu vaccine is the most effective way to reduce the risk of infection and severe illness.

Staying well hydrated is important for nearly every body function, including circulation, temperature control, and digestion. A common general guideline is to drink water throughout the day and to drink more during hot weather, exercise, or illness. Signs of dehydration include dark urine, dry mouth, dizziness, and reduced urination. Severe dehydration is a medical emergency and may require intravenous fluids.

Blood pressure is the force of blood pushing against the walls of the arteries. It is recorded as two numbers: systolic pressure over diastolic pressure. A reading around 120 over 80 mmHg is generally considered normal for adults. Consistently high blood pressure, known as hypertension, increases the risk of heart disease and stroke and often has no symptoms, which is why regular monitoring matters.

Hypertension, or high blood pressure, is frequently called a silent condition because many people have it without noticeable symptoms. Lifestyle measures such as reducing salt intake, staying physically active, maintaining a healthy weight, limiting alcohol, and not smoking can help lower blood pressure. When lifestyle changes are not enough, a clinician may prescribe medication that should be taken exactly as directed.

Type 2 diabetes is a condition in which the body either does not produce enough insulin or cannot use insulin effectively, leading to high blood sugar. Common early signs include increased thirst, frequent urination, fatigue, and blurred vision. Managing diabetes usually involves a balanced diet, regular physical activity, monitoring blood glucose, and sometimes medication. Uncontrolled diabetes can damage the eyes, kidneys, nerves, and heart over time.

Cholesterol is a waxy substance found in the blood. Low-density lipoprotein, or LDL, is often called the bad cholesterol because high levels can build up in the arteries. High-density lipoprotein, or HDL, is known as the good cholesterol because it helps remove other forms from the bloodstream. A heart-healthy diet, regular exercise, and avoiding trans fats support healthy cholesterol levels.

Vaccines work by training the immune system to recognize and fight specific viruses or bacteria without causing the disease itself. Routine immunizations protect against illnesses such as measles, tetanus, hepatitis, and influenza. Vaccination not only protects the individual but also helps protect the wider community through herd immunity. A healthcare provider can advise on the recommended vaccination schedule for each age group.

Sleep is essential for physical and mental health. Most adults need about seven to nine hours of quality sleep per night. Poor sleep is linked to weakened immunity, weight gain, difficulty concentrating, and a higher risk of chronic conditions. Good sleep habits include keeping a consistent schedule, limiting screen time before bed, avoiding caffeine late in the day, and keeping the bedroom dark and cool.

A balanced diet provides the nutrients the body needs to function well. It generally includes plenty of vegetables and fruits, whole grains, lean proteins, and healthy fats, while limiting added sugar, salt, and highly processed foods. Portion control and regular meal timing also support healthy weight and energy levels. Individual dietary needs can vary, so personalized advice from a dietitian can be helpful.

Regular physical activity benefits the heart, muscles, bones, and mood. General guidelines suggest at least 150 minutes of moderate aerobic activity per week, along with muscle-strengthening exercises on two or more days. Activity does not need to be intense to be beneficial; brisk walking, cycling, and swimming all count. People with existing health conditions should check with a clinician before starting a new exercise program.

Mental health is just as important as physical health. Feelings of stress, anxiety, and low mood are common, but persistent or severe symptoms deserve attention. Talking to a trusted person, maintaining routines, staying active, and seeking professional support can all help. If someone has thoughts of harming themselves, it is a medical emergency and immediate help should be sought from a crisis line or emergency services.

Paracetamol, also known as acetaminophen, is a common over-the-counter medication used to relieve pain and reduce fever. It is generally safe when taken at the recommended dose, but exceeding the maximum daily amount can cause serious liver damage. People should avoid combining multiple products that contain paracetamol and should consult a pharmacist if unsure about dosing.

Ibuprofen is a nonsteroidal anti-inflammatory drug, or NSAID, used to relieve pain, reduce inflammation, and lower fever. It is best taken with food to reduce the risk of stomach irritation. Ibuprofen may not be suitable for people with certain conditions such as stomach ulcers, kidney problems, or some heart conditions, so checking with a healthcare professional is advised.

Antibiotics treat infections caused by bacteria, not viruses. Using antibiotics when they are not needed, such as for a common cold, contributes to antibiotic resistance, which makes future infections harder to treat. When antibiotics are prescribed, it is important to complete the full course exactly as directed, even if symptoms improve before the medication is finished.

Allergies occur when the immune system reacts to a normally harmless substance such as pollen, dust, pet dander, or certain foods. Symptoms can range from sneezing and itchy eyes to skin rashes and, in severe cases, life-threatening anaphylaxis. Avoiding known triggers and using prescribed antihistamines can help manage mild allergies, while severe reactions require emergency care.

Asthma is a chronic condition in which the airways become inflamed and narrowed, causing wheezing, coughing, chest tightness, and shortness of breath. Triggers can include allergens, cold air, exercise, and respiratory infections. Asthma is managed with reliever inhalers for sudden symptoms and preventer inhalers for long-term control. A personalized asthma action plan from a clinician helps people respond to worsening symptoms.

Headaches are very common and usually not serious. Tension headaches often feel like a tight band around the head and may be linked to stress, poor posture, or dehydration. Migraines are more intense and can include throbbing pain, nausea, and sensitivity to light and sound. A headache that is sudden and severe, or that comes with confusion, weakness, or vision changes, requires urgent medical attention.

Knowing basic first aid can make a difference in an emergency. For a minor cut, the wound should be cleaned with water and covered with a clean dressing. For a burn, the area should be cooled under running water for several minutes. In any situation involving severe bleeding, difficulty breathing, chest pain, or loss of consciousness, emergency services should be called immediately.

Preventive health screenings help detect problems early, often before symptoms appear. Common screenings include blood pressure checks, cholesterol tests, blood sugar tests, and age-appropriate cancer screenings such as mammograms and colonoscopies. The recommended timing and frequency depend on age, sex, family history, and risk factors, and should be discussed with a healthcare provider.

Body mass index, or BMI, is a simple calculation using height and weight that estimates whether a person is in a healthy weight range. While BMI is a useful general screening tool, it does not directly measure body fat or account for muscle mass. A clinician can interpret BMI alongside other factors such as waist circumference and overall health.

Smoking is one of the leading causes of preventable disease, including lung cancer, heart disease, and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease. Quitting smoking at any age improves health and reduces risk over time. Support such as counseling, nicotine replacement therapy, and prescribed medications can significantly increase the chances of quitting successfully.

Drinking alcohol in excess can harm the liver, heart, and brain and increase the risk of several cancers. General guidance encourages keeping alcohol intake low and having alcohol-free days each week. People who are pregnant, taking certain medications, or managing specific health conditions may be advised to avoid alcohol entirely. A clinician can give individualized advice.

Maintaining good hand hygiene is one of the simplest and most effective ways to prevent the spread of infection. Washing hands with soap and water for at least twenty seconds removes germs, especially before eating, after using the toilet, and after coughing or sneezing. When soap and water are not available, an alcohol-based hand sanitizer is a reasonable alternative.

Dental health is closely connected to overall health. Brushing teeth twice a day with fluoride toothpaste, flossing daily, and having regular dental check-ups help prevent cavities and gum disease. Limiting sugary foods and drinks also protects the teeth. Persistent toothache, bleeding gums, or mouth sores should be evaluated by a dentist.

Eye health can be supported by regular eye examinations, protecting the eyes from excessive sun exposure, and taking breaks during prolonged screen use. Sudden vision loss, eye pain, flashes of light, or a curtain-like shadow across the vision are warning signs that require prompt medical assessment to prevent permanent damage.

Skin is the body's largest organ and acts as a protective barrier. Protecting skin from excessive sun exposure with sunscreen and clothing reduces the risk of skin cancer and premature aging. Any new, changing, or unusual mole, or a sore that does not heal, should be checked by a healthcare professional, as early detection of skin cancer greatly improves outcomes.

Stress is a natural response to demands and pressures, but ongoing high stress can affect both physical and mental health. It may contribute to headaches, sleep problems, high blood pressure, and low mood. Helpful strategies include regular exercise, relaxation techniques, adequate sleep, and connecting with supportive people. Professional help is valuable when stress feels overwhelming.

Pregnancy brings many changes to the body and often raises questions about nutrition, activity, and symptoms. Regular prenatal care helps monitor the health of both the pregnant person and the baby. Common recommendations include taking prenatal vitamins with folic acid, avoiding alcohol and smoking, and reporting warning signs such as severe bleeding or abdominal pain to a clinician promptly.

Childhood health includes routine vaccinations, regular growth and development checks, and good nutrition. Fever in young children is common, but parents should seek medical advice if an infant under three months has any fever, or if a child has a high fever, difficulty breathing, persistent vomiting, or appears unusually drowsy or unresponsive.

As people age, regular health check-ups become increasingly important for managing chronic conditions and maintaining independence. Staying physically and socially active, eating well, and reviewing medications with a clinician help support healthy aging. Older adults are also more vulnerable to falls, so reducing home hazards and maintaining strength and balance are valuable.

Nutrition labels provide useful information about the energy and nutrient content of foods. Paying attention to serving size, calories, added sugars, saturated fat, and sodium can support healthier choices. Choosing foods higher in fiber and lower in added sugar and salt is a practical way to improve everyday diet quality.

Diarrhea is the passage of loose or watery stools and is often caused by a viral or bacterial infection. Most cases resolve on their own within a few days. The main risk is dehydration, so drinking fluids and oral rehydration solutions is important. Medical care should be sought if there is blood in the stool, a high fever, signs of severe dehydration, or if symptoms persist.

Constipation refers to infrequent or difficult bowel movements. It is often related to low fiber intake, inadequate fluids, or lack of physical activity. Increasing fiber from fruits, vegetables, and whole grains, drinking enough water, and staying active usually help. Persistent constipation, especially with pain or blood, should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

Back pain is a very common complaint and is often caused by muscle strain, poor posture, or heavy lifting. Most cases improve with gentle movement, over-the-counter pain relief, and good posture. Prolonged bed rest is generally not recommended. Back pain that follows an injury, or that comes with numbness, leg weakness, or loss of bladder control, needs urgent assessment.

A sore throat is frequently caused by a viral infection and usually clears up within a week. Warm fluids, throat lozenges, and rest can ease discomfort. A sore throat with a high fever, white patches on the tonsils, or difficulty swallowing may indicate a bacterial infection such as strep throat, which a clinician can diagnose and treat if needed.

Coughs are a normal reflex that helps clear the airways. Most coughs are caused by viral infections and improve within a few weeks. Staying hydrated and using honey for adults and older children can soothe a cough. A cough that lasts more than three weeks, brings up blood, or comes with breathlessness or chest pain should be medically evaluated.

Urinary tract infections, or UTIs, are common, especially in women. Symptoms include a burning sensation when urinating, frequent urge to urinate, and cloudy or strong-smelling urine. Drinking plenty of fluids can help, but many UTIs require antibiotics prescribed by a clinician. Signs such as fever, back pain, or blood in the urine suggest a more serious infection.

Iron-deficiency anemia occurs when the body lacks enough iron to produce healthy red blood cells, leading to fatigue, weakness, and pale skin. It can result from poor diet, blood loss, or increased needs during pregnancy. Iron-rich foods and, when prescribed, iron supplements can help. A clinician can identify the underlying cause through blood tests.

Migraine management often combines avoiding known triggers, maintaining regular sleep and meals, staying hydrated, and using prescribed medications. Some people benefit from resting in a dark, quiet room during an attack. Keeping a headache diary can help identify patterns and triggers. Frequent or worsening migraines should be reviewed by a healthcare professional.

Vitamin D supports bone health by helping the body absorb calcium. The body produces vitamin D through sunlight exposure, and it is also found in some foods and supplements. People with limited sun exposure, older adults, and those with certain conditions may be at higher risk of deficiency. A clinician can advise whether testing or supplementation is appropriate.

Heart attack warning signs can include chest pain or pressure, pain spreading to the arm, neck, or jaw, shortness of breath, sweating, and nausea. Symptoms can differ between individuals and may be less obvious in women and older adults. A suspected heart attack is a medical emergency, and emergency services should be called immediately rather than waiting to see if symptoms pass.

Stroke is a medical emergency that occurs when blood flow to part of the brain is interrupted. The FAST acronym helps recognize the signs: Face drooping, Arm weakness, Speech difficulty, and Time to call emergency services. Acting quickly is critical because early treatment can reduce long-term damage. Any sudden weakness, numbness, or confusion should be treated as urgent.

Managing a healthy weight involves a balance between the energy taken in through food and the energy used through activity. Gradual, sustainable changes to diet and physical activity are more effective than extreme diets. Setting realistic goals and seeking support from a healthcare provider or dietitian can improve long-term success and overall health.

Caffeine is a stimulant found in coffee, tea, and many soft and energy drinks. Moderate amounts are fine for most adults, but too much can cause restlessness, a fast heartbeat, and difficulty sleeping. Sensitivity to caffeine varies, and people who are pregnant or have certain heart conditions may be advised to limit their intake.

Sun protection helps prevent sunburn, premature skin aging, and skin cancer. Practical steps include using a broad-spectrum sunscreen, wearing protective clothing and a hat, seeking shade during peak hours, and avoiding tanning beds. Sunscreen should be reapplied regularly, especially after swimming or sweating.

Probiotics are live microorganisms that may support gut health, and they are found in foods such as yogurt and fermented products. While some people find them helpful, evidence varies depending on the specific strain and condition. Anyone considering probiotic supplements, especially those with weakened immune systems, should discuss it with a healthcare professional first.

Seasonal allergies, sometimes called hay fever, are triggered by airborne pollens and cause sneezing, a runny nose, and itchy eyes. Keeping windows closed during high pollen periods, showering after being outdoors, and using prescribed or recommended antihistamines can reduce symptoms. Persistent or severe symptoms may benefit from review by a clinician.

Dehydration during exercise can reduce performance and, in hot conditions, lead to heat-related illness. Drinking water before, during, and after activity helps maintain hydration. For prolonged or intense exercise, drinks containing electrolytes may be useful. Warning signs of heat illness include dizziness, nausea, and confusion, which require prompt cooling and medical attention.

Good posture supports the spine and can reduce neck and back strain, particularly for people who sit for long periods. Setting up a workstation so the screen is at eye level, the feet are flat, and the back is supported can help. Taking regular breaks to stand and move is also beneficial for circulation and comfort.

Managing chronic conditions such as diabetes, hypertension, or asthma usually involves a combination of medication, lifestyle measures, and regular monitoring. Keeping a record of symptoms and readings, attending follow-up appointments, and taking medications as prescribed all contribute to better control. Patients should never stop prescribed medication without consulting their clinician.

Mental wellbeing can be supported by maintaining social connections, engaging in meaningful activities, getting regular exercise, and practicing relaxation techniques. Setting realistic goals and asking for help when needed are signs of strength, not weakness. Persistent feelings of sadness, anxiety, or hopelessness should be discussed with a healthcare professional.

Over-the-counter medications can be effective for minor ailments, but they still carry risks if misused. Reading the label, following dosing instructions, checking for interactions with other medicines, and asking a pharmacist when unsure are all good practices. People with chronic conditions or who are pregnant should confirm that a medication is safe for them.

Knowing when to seek emergency care can save lives. Warning signs that require immediate attention include difficulty breathing, chest pain, sudden severe headache, signs of stroke, severe bleeding, a serious allergic reaction, or loss of consciousness. When in doubt about a serious symptom, it is safer to contact emergency services or seek urgent medical care.
